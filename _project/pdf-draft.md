# A Note Before You Start

I want to be straight with you before we get into any of this.

I am not a PGA professional. I do not teach golf for a living. What I am is a golfer who spent most of his life as a high handicapper — frustrated, inconsistent, and convinced that the next lesson was going to be the one that finally made it click.

It never did. Not completely.

Over years of lessons, range sessions, and more trial and error than I care to admit, I slowly pieced together what actually works. Not theory. Not textbook mechanics. Real fundamentals — a small set of things that, when you get them right and do them consistently, make the swing feel natural. Simple. Repeatable.

I wrote this guide because I wished it had existed when I started. Not a collection of tips. Not another list of things to think about over the ball. A connected system — one that explains the fundamentals in the order they build on each other, from the ground up.

**This is that guide.**

---

## What This Guide Is

This is an irons guide. Only irons. That is intentional.

Most golfers obsess over their driver. I understand — it is the most fun club in the bag. But your irons are where your score actually comes from. In a typical round you will hit your irons on every approach shot, every par-3, and most layup situations. Your driver? Maybe fourteen swings. Fix the club you actually use.

Inside you will find seven core lessons, a five-session range plan, and a simple pre-shot checklist. Everything is connected. Each lesson builds on the last. Read the guide once from start to finish, then take it to the range.

---

## What This Guide Is Not

This is not a promise that you will shoot par. It is not a miracle fix. It is not a shortcut around the work that golf requires.

Golf takes practice. Real practice — deliberate, focused, repetitive. The fundamentals in this guide are simple, but simple does not mean easy. Getting a correct grip to feel natural takes hundreds of repetitions. Building a consistent body turn takes dedicated range sessions. There are no shortcuts.

What this guide does is point you in the right direction so that the work you put in actually produces results — instead of grooving the same bad habits over and over.

---

## The Right Mindset Going In

Here is what I want you to hold onto as you work through this:

**You are not trying to shoot par.** You are trying to get better. Those are very different goals, and the second one is far more achievable and far more enjoyable.

Every golfer improves at a different pace. Some concepts will click immediately. Others will take weeks to feel right. That is completely normal and has nothing to do with your ability or your potential.

The goal is simple progress. Hit the ball a little more consistently this month than last month. Miss a little less to the right. Make better contact on your approach shots. Lower your handicap by a few strokes over the season. That kind of improvement — steady, real, earned — is what makes this game worth playing.

**And through all of it, have fun.** Golf is a game. The range should be enjoyable. The course should be enjoyable. If it stops being fun, you are working too hard and thinking too much. Which, coincidentally, is exactly what this guide is designed to fix.

---

## A Note on Terminology

Throughout this guide I use the terms **lead hand** and **trail hand** rather than left and right. Your lead hand is the hand closest to the target — the left hand for right-handed golfers, the right hand for left-handed golfers. Your trail hand is the hand furthest from the target.

I am a right-handed golfer. When I describe positions and feelings, picture them from that perspective. But every fundamental in this guide applies equally to left-handed players — simply mirror the directions.

---

## How to Use This Guide

1. **Read it once** from start to finish before you go to the range. Understand the system before you try to apply any piece of it.
2. **Take it with you.** Print it or keep it on your phone. Reference it during range sessions.

# Lesson 1: Grip That Controls the Clubface

## Why Grip Comes First

If you take nothing else from this guide, take this: the grip is the most
important fundamental in golf. Not the swing. Not your stance. The grip.

Here is why. The clubface controls where the ball goes. And your hands are
the only connection between you and the clubface. If that connection is
wrong, nothing else you do — no swing thought, no drill, no adjustment —
will produce consistent results. You are literally building on a broken
foundation.

The other thing worth saying upfront: the correct grip often feels awkward.
Especially at first. Especially if you have been gripping the club the wrong
way for years. That awkward feeling is not a sign that something is wrong.
It is a sign that something is finally right. Stick with it.

There is also a mechanical reason the grip matters beyond just holding the
club. A correct grip works naturally with the rotation of your forearms
through the swing to return the clubface to square at impact. You do not
have to think about squaring the face — a correct grip does it for you. A
weak or incorrect grip fights the swing and forces you to make compensations
that are nearly impossible to repeat consistently.

---

## The Process

This is how I build my grip every single time I step up to the ball. Not
sometimes. Every time. After thousands of repetitions the process becomes
faster — almost automatic — but the process never changes. That consistency
is exactly the point.

### Step 1 — Start With a Square Face

Hold the club out in front of you with your trail hand, arm extended, and
look at the face. Make sure it is square — not open, not closed. This is
your reference point. Everything that follows is built around a square face.

Do not skip this step. It takes two seconds and it anchors everything else.
Once you have built your grip thousands of times this step becomes less
necessary — but until then, start here every time.

### Step 2 — Lead Hand

Bring your lead hand under the club. Place the grip diagonally across your
fingers — not in the palm, across the fingers. This matters. A grip held
in the palm eliminates your ability to hinge the wrists correctly and kills
feel through the shot.

Once your fingers are positioned, wrap your hand around the club and bring
your thumb to rest on top of the shaft. I personally like the feeling of
extending my lead thumb slightly down the shaft — it creates a more secure
connection and gives me a reference point I can feel consistently.

**Check:** The V formed between your lead thumb and index finger should
point toward your lead shoulder. If it is pointing toward your chin or your
trail shoulder, your grip is too weak and the face will tend to open at
impact.

### Step 3 — Trail Hand

Place your trail hand on the club so that your trail pinky finger sits over
the gap between your lead index finger and middle finger. You can overlap it
or interlock it — either works. What matters is that the hands feel
connected and unified, not independent.

Turn your trail hand over the lead so that your lead thumb sits in the
pocket of your trail palm. The two hands should feel like one unit working
together.

Two things I focus on specifically with my trail hand:

**The trigger finger.** My trail index finger sits slightly separated from
the others, bent — like I am pulling a trigger. This promotes feel and
control through impact.

**The last two fingers.** I maintain light, firm tension in the ring finger
and pinky of my trail hand against the shaft. Not a tight grip. Deliberate,
connected tension. These two fingers are your anchor. Feel them on every
swing.

**Check:** The V formed between your trail thumb and index finger should
point toward your trail shoulder. Both Vs — lead and trail — pointing in
their respective directions tells you the grip is correct.

---

## How Tight to Grip

Firm enough that the club will not twist in your hands at impact. Not so
tight that you feel tension in your forearms. If your forearms are tense,
you are gripping too hard.

Think of a scale of one to ten. You want a five or six. Secure, not
strangled. Tension in the hands and forearms restricts the natural rotation
of the arms through the swing — the exact rotation that squares the face at
impact.

---

## Same Grip for Every Club

One of the most damaging habits in recreational golf is adjusting the grip
between clubs. Do not do this. Build one grip. Use it for every iron, every
shot, every time. The swing adapts slightly between clubs — the grip does not.

---

## Practice Until It Is Automatic

The grip is a physical skill. Reading about it is not enough. You need to
build it the same way, in the same sequence, until the process becomes
ingrained.

- Grip and re-grip the club while watching television
- Before every shot at the range, build the grip from Step 1
- Check your Vs after building the grip until checking becomes unnecessary

Do not rush to the next lesson until building a correct grip feels natural.
Not perfect — natural. This foundation supports everything that follows.

---

## The Most Common Mistake

A grip that is too weak — hands rotated too far toward the target — is the
single most common grip error in recreational golf. A weak grip leaves the
face open at impact. An open face at impact produces slices, pushes, and
loss of distance.

If you have been fighting a slice for years, start here. Strengthen the
grip — rotate both hands slightly away from the target until both Vs point
where they should. It will feel wrong. Hit balls anyway. The ball flight
will tell you everything you need to know.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Start with a square face** — every single time, non-negotiable
- **Lead hand:** diagonal across fingers, V toward lead shoulder, thumb extended slightly down shaft
- **Trail hand:** over lead thumb, V toward trail shoulder
- **Trigger finger** on trail index finger for feel and control
- **Last two fingers** of trail hand — light, firm tension
- **Same grip** for every club, every shot
- **Practice the process** until it is automatic — not just familiar

# Lesson 2: Setup & Distance — The Contact Foundation

## Why Setup Matters More Than You Think

Here is something most golfers never fully accept: the quality of your
contact is largely determined before you swing the club. Your setup — how
you stand to the ball, where the ball is in relation to your body, where
it sits in your stance — sets the conditions for everything that follows.

Get the setup right and the swing has a chance. Get it wrong and you are
compensating before you even take the club back.

The good news is that setup is the most controllable part of the game. You
are standing still. You have time. There is no excuse for a poor setup once
you know what you are looking for.

---

## The Natural Athletic Position

The single most important concept in setup is this: **natural.**

You are not reaching for the ball. You are not pulling the club tight against
your body. You are finding the position where your body is balanced,
your arms hang freely, and the club meets the ball without strain.

Here is how to find it:

Stand upright holding the club. Take a very slight bend in the knees — not
a deep squat, just a soft, athletic flex. Lean forward from the hips — not
the waist — until your arms hang loosely in front of you. Stop there.

That is your position. Wherever your hands fall naturally is where they
belong. The ball should be directly under the clubhead. If you feel like
you are reaching, you are too far away. If you feel cramped, you are too
close. Natural is the target.

Most recreational golfers stand too far from the ball. They reach out to
it, which pulls the arms away from the body and forces an arm-dominated
swing — the exact thing that kills consistency. When you move closer, it
often feels too close at first. That feeling is normal. Trust it.

---

## Ball Position in the Stance

This is one of the more nuanced aspects of setup and one that most
recreational golfers either ignore or get completely wrong.

The ball does not sit in the same spot in your stance for every iron. It
moves — and understanding where it should be for each club is one of the
things that will immediately improve your contact.

Here is the principle: **your hands stay in the same natural position.
The ball moves. Your hands do not.**

### High-Lofted Irons (Pitching Wedge, 9-iron, 8-iron)

For your most lofted clubs, the ball sits approximately three-quarters of
the way toward your trail foot — back in your stance. Not all the way back,
but noticeably behind center.

With the ball in this position and your hands in their natural spot, your
hands will be slightly ahead of the ball at address. This is intentional
and important. Hands ahead of the ball at address promotes a slightly
descending strike — exactly what you want with high-lofted irons to
create clean contact and proper spin.

### Mid Irons (7-iron, 6-iron)

As the club gets longer and less lofted, the ball moves progressively
forward in your stance — toward the center. Your 7-iron sits between the
three-quarter position and center. Your 6-iron is close to center.

Your hands remain slightly ahead of the ball throughout.

### Longer Irons (5-iron)

Your 5-iron plays at or near the center of your stance. Hands still
slightly ahead. The progression from short to long irons is gradual —
not a dramatic jump at any single club.

**The key principle to remember:** the ball position changes as you move
through the bag. Your hand position does not. Hands slightly ahead of the
ball is a constant.

---

## Stance Width

Stance width is less critical than the other elements of setup — especially
when you are first building these habits. Do not let it become a distraction.

A general guideline: feet roughly shoulder-width apart. If you are unsure,
err on the narrower side rather than wider. A wider stance can restrict the
hip rotation you need for a full turn. A narrower stance forces you to rely
on good mechanics rather than a wide base.

One practical note: deliberately narrowing your stance is actually a useful
drill when your swing is getting too quick. A narrow stance makes it very
difficult to swing hard without losing balance — which naturally slows
things down. We will come back to this in the tempo lesson.

---

## Minor Adjustments That Make a Major Difference

Two small adjustments at address that have a significant effect on the swing:

**Right shoulder slightly back.** At address, your trail shoulder sits
very slightly back from your lead shoulder. This is natural given that
your trail hand is lower on the club — but consciously reinforcing it
promotes the correct swing path and encourages the body turn we will
cover in the next lesson.

**Trail foot slightly back.** Your trail foot sits just slightly behind
your lead foot — perhaps an inch or two. This subtle open stance creates
space for the hips to rotate through the swing without restriction.

Neither of these adjustments is dramatic. You are not opening your stance
significantly. Small adjustments with meaningful effects.

---

## The Setup Routine

Like the grip, setup needs to be built the same way every time. Here is
a simple routine:

1. Build your grip before you address the ball
2. Stand to the ball — find the natural hanging position
3. Check ball distance — not reaching, not cramped
4. Check ball position in the stance for the club you are hitting
5. Trail shoulder slightly back, trail foot slightly back
6. One look at the target

That is your setup. It takes ten seconds. Do it the same way every time.

---

## The Most Common Mistake

Reaching for the ball. It is the most common setup error in recreational
golf and one of the most damaging.

When you reach, your arms extend away from your body. An arms-dominated
setup leads to an arms-dominated swing — where the arms do the work
instead of the body. The result is inconsistent contact, loss of power,
and a swing that is nearly impossible to repeat.

The fix is simple: move closer to the ball. Let your arms hang. Trust
the natural position. It will feel different. Different is fine. Hit balls
from the new position and the results will do the convincing.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Natural athletic position** — slight knee flex, lean from hips, arms hang freely
- **Ball distance** — closer than you think, not reaching
- **Ball position** — back in stance for short irons, progressively forward to center for longer irons
- **Hands slightly ahead** of the ball at address — consistent across all irons
- **Stance width** — roughly shoulder width, err narrower if unsure
- **Trail shoulder and trail foot** — slightly back at address
- **Same routine** every time — setup is controllable, treat it that way

# PART THREE: THE PRE-SHOT CHECKLIST

**Before every shot — on the range and on the course:**

1. **Grip correct?** Lead hand V toward lead shoulder. Trail hand V toward trail shoulder. Thumbs together.
2. **Ball close — not reaching?** Arms hang naturally. Not extended, not cramped.
3. **Shoulder and foot slightly back?** Trail shoulder and trail foot just slightly behind neutral.
4. **Committed to a full turn?** One swing thought. Full body rotation. Body leads, arms follow.
